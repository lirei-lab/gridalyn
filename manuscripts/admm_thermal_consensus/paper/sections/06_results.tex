\section{Results}

\subsection{Aggregate coordination}
Figure~\ref{fig:profiles} shows the cold-day aggregate load. The uncoordinated
profile exhibits the classic double peak --- a morning and an evening heating
surge --- whereas the ideal ADMM coordination flattens it to a nearly constant
$\approx 147.7$~kW. The $\varrho = 0.8$ trace, in which most agents are imputed,
recovers much of the flattening but leaves residual peaks where imputation error
accumulates.

ADMM-ideal profile ($\approx 147.7$~kW) and the $\varrho = 0.8$ imputed case with

\label{fig:profiles}

Table~\ref{tab:agg} quantifies the aggregate key performance indicators. Ideal
coordination cuts the peak from 169.1 to 147.7~kW, a 12.7\% reduction, and drives
the peak-to-average ratio (PAR) from 1.146 down to 1.001 --- essentially a flat
load. A PAR of 1.001 is the practical floor: it means the coordinated aggregate
is constant to within a tenth of a percent across the entire day, so the
flattening objective in~\eqref{eq:sharing} has been met almost exactly. Energy
cost falls slightly, from 371.0 to 364.8~CAD --- a 1.66\% reduction --- because
the daily-energy constraint conserves total heating; the saving comes from
shifting load out of the evening-peak TOU window (0.158~CAD/kWh) toward off-peak
hours (0.078~CAD/kWh) rather than from using less energy. The modest size of the
cost reduction is itself a useful result, and an honest one: the objective
in~\eqref{eq:sharing} flattens the aggregate (it penalizes departure from a
\emph{constant} level), it does not chase the TOU price signal. A purely flat
profile still places substantial load in the priced evening window, so the bill
moves only as a side effect of peak-shaving. With energy conserved, demand
response of this kind buys \emph{capacity} relief (a lower peak) far more than it
buys energy savings, and the case for it must rest on the network benefit rather
than the bill.

\subsubsection{Comfort preservation}
The flattening is achieved without sacrificing thermal comfort. The mean absolute
heating deviation of the ideal coordinated schedule from the thermostat baseline
is 0.298~kW per home-step --- small against the $\approx 5$~kW heating level the
homes operate at. This is comfort preserved \emph{by construction}: the
projection~\eqref{eq:proj} keeps every step inside the $\pm 50\%$ band of its
baseline and conserves each home's daily heating energy, so the coordinator can
only re-time heat, never withhold it. The result is a feeder-level flat profile
that is, at the level of each individual home, only a gentle nudge of when the
thermostat draws power.

The imputed scenarios track the ideal closely up to $\varrho = 0.6$ and only degrade
materially at $\varrho = 0.8$, where the peak rises to 159.4~kW and PAR to 1.080.
The per-scenario imputation RMSE (last column) is non-monotone in $\varrho$: it
grows from 0.170 to 0.252~kW as the imputed population grows, then dips to
0.234~kW at $\varrho = 0.8$. This is an averaging effect --- with 24 of 30 agents
imputed, individual errors partially cancel in the aggregate --- and it explains
why the aggregate-level damage at $\varrho = 0.8$ comes less from raw imputation
error than from the loss of the responsive agents whose true, flexible schedules
were doing most of the flattening work.

\subsection{Imputation accuracy and robustness}
The LightGBM imputer is a competent forecaster of an unseen home's heating. Under
5-fold leave-agents-out cross-validation it achieves an RMSE of 0.294~kW and an
MAE of 0.247~kW, against a heating standard deviation of 0.575~kW. At roughly
51\% of the natural variability, the imputer is clearly better than predicting
the mean and is accurate enough that the coordinated aggregate barely moves for
small non-responsive fractions, as seen in the per-scenario RMSE column of
Table~\ref{tab:agg}.

Figure~\ref{fig:peakpar} plots peak and PAR against $\varrho$. Both stay essentially
flat through $\varrho = 0.6$ and then turn sharply upward at $\varrho = 0.8$, the
signature of a robustness threshold rather than a gradual decay.

A secondary finding concerns tractability rather than outcome quality. The ADMM
iteration count grows steeply with $\varrho$: 8 iterations for the ideal case, then
14, 21, 150, and 280 for $\varrho = 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8$ respectively (all runs
converged). Communication failure thus degrades coordination \emph{tractability}
--- the effort to reach consensus --- well before it degrades the coordinated
\emph{outcome}, an early-warning signal an operator could monitor.

The shape of this curve is informative. From $\varrho = 0.0$ to $\varrho = 0.4$ the
iteration count roughly doubles per step (8, 14, 21), an almost linear growth
that the coordinator absorbs easily. Between $\varrho = 0.4$ and $\varrho = 0.6$ it
jumps by a factor of seven (21 to 150), and by $\varrho = 0.8$ it reaches 280 ---
35 times the ideal effort. The knee of this curve sits at the same place as the
feasibility knee in the network results below, near $\varrho = 0.6$. The two are
linked: as more agents are imputed, the aggregate the coordinator sees diverges
from the aggregate that physically forms, the sharing residual becomes harder to
drive to zero, and convergence slows. Iteration count is therefore a cheap,
purely software-side proxy for the harder-to-observe physical-feasibility margin,
because both respond to the same underlying quantity --- the mismatch injected by
imputation.

Read as a sequence of effects, the $8 \rightarrow 280$ growth says that
communication failure degrades the \emph{solver} before it degrades the
\emph{outcome}. The coordinator must work thirty-five times harder at
$\varrho = 0.8$ than in the ideal case to reconcile an aggregate that no longer
matches reality, yet the aggregate it eventually produces is still only mildly
worse (a 159.4~kW peak against the ideal 147.7~kW) until the very last sweep
point. Convergence effort is thus a leading indicator: it rises while outcomes
are still acceptable, giving an operator a purely computational signal --- visible
before any meter or power-flow check --- that the coordination problem is becoming
ill-conditioned and a feasibility breach may follow.

\subsection{Network validation}
The closed-loop contribution is summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:network} and
Table~\ref{tab:net}. The uncoordinated aggregate loads the worst trunk line to
115\% --- a thermal violation --- and drops the worst minimum voltage to
0.905~p.u. Ideal coordination \emph{clears} the thermal overload: maximum line
loading falls to 99.7\% (no violation) and worst minimum voltage rises to
0.918~p.u. This is the core result: flattening the aggregate is not merely a
statistical improvement, it restores feeder feasibility.

The cleared state is resilient to communication failure up to $\varrho = 0.6$, where
the worst line rides the 100.0\% edge but does not violate. At $\varrho = 0.8$ the
accumulated imputation error re-introduces a 108\% overload --- the feeder
re-violates. The story is therefore $115\% \rightarrow 99.7\% \rightarrow 108\%$:
coordination clears the overload, the clearance survives substantial
non-responsiveness, and only collapses once most agents must be imputed. This
identifies a critical non-responsive fraction between 0.6 and 0.8 beyond which
imputation can no longer protect the feeder.

\paragraph{An honest note on voltage.} We report voltage as a \emph{severity}
measure, not a compliance verdict. On the \texttt{case33bw} benchmark the worst
minimum voltage never reaches the 0.95~p.u. ANSI limit in any scenario --- the
feeder is inherently stressed --- so we do not claim voltage compliance.
Coordination nonetheless reduces under-voltage \emph{depth} (0.905 to
0.918~p.u.), which is a genuine improvement in feeder condition. The clean
cleared/re-violated headline comes from line loading, where the 100\% thermal
limit gives an unambiguous binary verdict.

\subsection{Discussion}
Three observations tie the results together. First, the aggregate, tractability,
and feasibility curves all share the same knee near $\varrho = 0.6$: the iteration
count, the peak, and the worst line loading all stay benign through $\varrho = 0.6$
and break together at $\varrho = 0.8$. This suggests that a single observable
quantity --- the divergence between the imputed and the realized aggregate ---
governs all three, and that an operator monitoring ADMM convergence effort has an
early proxy for impending feeder violation. Second, the network result is not a
restatement of the aggregate result: a 12.7\% peak cut is a statistical
improvement, but \emph{clearing a 115\% overload to 99.7\%} is an operational
state change from infeasible to feasible, and the two need not coincide on a
different feeder or load allocation. Third, the failure at $\varrho = 0.8$ is graceful
rather than catastrophic --- 108\% loading, not a non-converging power flow ---
which indicates that the imputer keeps the system in a recoverable regime even
when most agents are silent. Together these support the central claim that
coordination quality should be measured by feeder feasibility, and that
imputation extends the range of communication failure over which that feasibility
holds.
